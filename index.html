3 Key Thematic Angles for In-Class Discussion

Discussion Guide
Angle 1 Chapters 18 & 19

Courage vs. Conformity: Truth in an Unjust Courtroom

Core Insight: While Mayella Ewell conforms to her father's lie out of fear and shame, Tom Robinson and Link Deas display moral courage by speaking truthfully even when honesty violates Maycomb's racial expectations.

Discussion Talking Points:
  • How does Mayella's confusion over Atticus calling her "Miss" and "Ma'am" reveal the isolation and abuse of her home life?
  • Why was Tom Robinson trapped in an impossible dilemma inside the Ewell cabin—unable to strike a white woman yet appearing guilty by running?
  • What risk does Link Deas take by standing up in court to vouch for Tom's eight years of honest work?
Angle 2 Chapters 19 & 20

The Unspoken Social Code & Why Pity Offends Maycomb

Core Insight: Tom Robinson seals his fate not because of any physical evidence, but because admitting he "felt sorry" for a white woman upends Maycomb's rigid racial hierarchy.

Discussion Talking Points:
  • Why does Mr. Gilmer immediately pounce on Tom's words: "You felt sorry for her, you felt sorry for her?"
  • How does Atticus explain Mayella's guilt—breaking a "time-honored code" by tempting a Black man and then trying to destroy "her daily reminder of what she did"?
  • What does Dolphus Raymond mean when he tells Scout that Dill cries "about the hell white people give colored folks, without even stopping to think that they're people, too"?
Angle 3 Chapter 20

The Rhetoric of Justice: Atticus's Closing Argument

Core Insight: By shedding his formal coat and vest and invoking Thomas Jefferson's maxim "All men are created equal," Atticus challenges the twelve jurors to rise above prejudice and honor their civic duty.

Discussion Talking Points:
  • Why does Atticus unbutton his vest and remove his coat before addressing the jury as if "talking to folks on the post office corner"?
  • What double meaning lies behind Atticus's statement, "This case is as simple as black and white"?
  • Why does Atticus call American courts "the great levelers" while warning that "a court is only as sound as its jury"?

Exemplar Analytical Response (230 Words): Theme: Rhetoric of Justice & Moral Courage (Ch. 20)

In Chapter 20 of To Kill a Mockingbird, Harper Lee uses Atticus Finch's closing argument to dismantle Maycomb's racial hypocrisy and place the burden of justice squarely on the individual consciences of the jurors. Before speaking, Atticus unbuttons his vest and removes his coat, shedding courtroom formality to address the twelve men "as if they were folks on the post office corner." Through this deliberate shift in ethos, Atticus appeals to them not as distant elites, but as ordinary neighbors capable of moral clarity. He systematically exposes the prosecution's reliance on the "cynical confidence" that the jury will automatically accept a white family's word over a Black man's, bluntly declaring that "this case is as simple as black and white." By invoking Thomas Jefferson's principle that "all men are created equal" and calling the courtroom "the great leveler" where a pauper stands equal to a Rockefeller, Atticus elevates Tom Robinson's trial into a test of American democratic ideals. Ultimately, Atticus's warning that "a court is only as sound as its jury" reveals Lee's central conviction: legal institutions cannot manufacture justice on their own unless ordinary citizens possess the courage to set aside ingrained prejudice.
